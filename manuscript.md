# The Neurology Neuropathy Illusion

## I. Before Neuropathy

The first surviving GP entry does not begin with a neurological theory. It begins with feet. On 10 April 2017, Liam reports bilateral foot pain and an abnormal gait. He thinks the gait may have begun with an old left-sided sciatica problem and may now be partly habitual, although he has no current back or leg pain. Examination adds findings that are not merely subjective: marked mallet toes, onychogryphosis, callouses and pressure areas. He works on his feet and is struggling. Podiatry is planned, along with blood tests including diabetes screening. At this date, the problem has shape, but not yet an organising neurological explanation.

Those structural findings need to remain exactly what they are. They are not decorative details waiting for a later neurological diagnosis to make them unimportant. Nor do they establish that the feet belong to an entirely separate process. A callus can be real without explaining why weight was being borne in a particular way. A malformed toe can be clinically significant without deciding what produced or aggravated its posture. The April entry does not need to answer those questions. Its value is earlier and simpler: it records a painful, visibly abnormal pair of feet alongside an abnormal gait before any specialist has divided the presentation into explanatory compartments. The relationship among those facts is still open.

Podiatry was therefore a legitimate response to what was visible. Later records preserve marked structural and biomechanical abnormalities: corn and callus, rearfoot or arch pain, forefoot pain, toe deformity and gait abnormality. Subsequent Podiatry treatment documented a callus at the left fifth metatarsophalangeal joint, toe props for the second to fourth toes bilaterally, and tight gastrocnemius and soleus muscles for which stretching was prescribed. None of this requires a hidden neurological mechanism to make it clinically real. But neither does the appropriateness of Podiatry settle the relationship between the feet and the gait. Treatment of structural pathology and explanation of the larger presentation are different tasks.

By 8 June, the record has begun to change. The GP now notices difficulty with the fluid initiation of movement. Liam describes trying to time his movements on a bus so that the vehicle slowing helps propel him forward when he gets up. The GP is considering a Neurology opinion not simply for gait, but for his “gait, feet and ‘muscle tension’ issues.” The wording matters because it preserves the problem before specialist allocation. Feet, gait and tension are still being carried together. The record does not yet say that one belongs to Podiatry and another to Neurology. It says that their relationship is becoming harder to ignore.

Four days later, occupational-health concern reinforces the GP's own. A Neurology referral is to proceed even before the older notes arrive. That escalation matters because it shows the level at which the problem is changing. The GP is no longer only managing painful feet or visible deformity. Another clinician has independently seen enough in the gait to echo concern, and the absence of historical records is no longer considered reason to delay specialist assessment. The surviving source does not tell us what the ultimate explanation should be. It does show that the clinical object is enlarging. Local foot pathology remains present, but it is increasingly difficult to treat the gait as a simple consequence of discomfort or habit.

On 23 June, the neurological content becomes more concrete. The GP records marked increased tone in both legs and brisk reflexes in the upper limbs. Liam has been drinking on most nights to help “relax muscles.” That alcohol history will later acquire greater explanatory weight, but it should not be allowed to arrive early in its later role. Here it is first carried as part of the patient's response to tension. Alcohol is a historical fact and a health concern. It is not yet a documented explanation for the feet. The distinction matters because the same fact can change function without changing its wording. Later causation should not be mistaken for earlier provenance.

The urgent referral of 29 June brings the developing problem into one document. The GP recalls that Liam transferred to the practice in April with an unusual festinant and stiff gait while attending with bilateral foot issues, which he had attributed at that time to callouses and corns. Over the following weeks, however, trouble initiating movements had become increasingly evident. There was marked stiffness and increased tone in both upper and lower limbs, brisk reflexes and possible cogwheeling at the wrist. The referral preserves both levels at once: the patient's earlier explanation and the GP's increasingly neurological observations. It does not require either to disappear.

Toe clawing appears inside that same unresolved presentation. Liam describes being unable to relax his muscles, particularly in bed, where he claws his toes and cannot become comfortable. This is an important detail precisely because it has not yet been assigned a mechanism. The source does not call it dystonia. It does not call it neuropathy. It does not say that the clawing causes the foot pain, or that the damaged feet cause the clawing. It records abnormal toe posture in a patient whose lower and upper limb tone is increasing. The temptation to sort those findings into later diagnostic boxes belongs to hindsight. The referral itself leaves the relationship available for investigation.

The clinical problem also moves beyond gait. Liam is having increasing difficulty at work, particularly with gowning and ungowning around the clean room. His dexterity is worse. Tasks that had previously been straightforward—sorting papers, fitting scalpel blades to handles, repetitive manual work—have become difficult. That widening matters because it weakens any account in which the whole presentation can be reduced to painful feet producing an odd walk. The feet remain important, but the body is now presenting a broader movement problem. What the feet mean inside that wider problem is not yet known. Their persistence does not become less significant merely because new neurological abnormalities have appeared elsewhere.

The GP's conclusion is correspondingly open. There are definite signs of increased tone, abnormal gait, functional deterioration and declining employment capacity. The question entered in the referral is “?parkinsonian or other underlying diagnosis.” That question mark should survive the reader's hindsight. It does not mean that every possibility was equally likely. It means the referral had not yet completed the diagnostic task. Parkinsonism was becoming a serious concern, but it was still an inquiry rather than inherited fact. More importantly for the feet, the referral does not present Parkinsonism as one problem and an established second condition as another. It sends an integrated but unresolved presentation forward for specialist assessment.

Alcohol is also present in the referral, but again its role should be kept on its original scale. The GP records that Liam had taken alcohol, perhaps too much in the past, and elsewhere records its current use to relax muscles. The later specialist history would describe a period of four to five pints per day, taken to allow sleep and relaxation. These are clinically relevant facts. They can legitimately provoke questions about neurological or nutritional effects. What they do not yet establish is a causal pathway from alcohol to the feet. That pathway, if it is to enter the account later, must be made rather than silently presumed to have existed all along.

Immediately before specialist Neurology, then, several things are already clear. The feet are genuinely abnormal. The pain is bilateral. The gait is unusual. Movement initiation is impaired. Tone is increased. Reflexes are brisk. Toes claw. Dexterity is deteriorating. Employment is threatened. Alcohol exists in the history. Podiatry is already part of the pathway. What is not clear is how these findings belong together. The record has not yet demonstrated that the foot pain is independent of the movement disorder, nor that structural pathology alone accounts for the gait, nor that one neurological mechanism explains every feature. Unresolvedness here is not absence of information. It is information without a settled relationship.

This is also where the limits of the record itself need to remain visible. A GP note can establish what was recorded on a particular date, what the GP observed, what Liam was reported as saying and what action followed. It cannot transparently reproduce every sensation, examination manoeuvre or conversation that occurred. The referral is similarly strong evidence of the information sent to the hospital, but not a complete transcript of the clinical reality being referred. That distinction will matter later when more elaborate propositions appear. For now, it prevents a simpler mistake: assuming that because something is absent from a surviving note it was necessarily absent from experience, or from clinical thought.

At the same time, missing documentation cannot be repaired with imagination. The disclosed pre-specialist material does not show a peripheral-neuropathy diagnosis. It does not show a demonstrated sensory-loss pattern establishing neuropathy. It does not show neurophysiology. Those statements define the evidential limit of the surviving material; they do not prove that no sensory question was ever asked or that no unrecorded thought occurred. The difference is essential. A later explanation cannot borrow certainty from hypothetical undocumented work, just as the manuscript cannot convert documentary absence into proof that work never happened. Before specialist synthesis, the safe statement is narrower: the visible problem remains feet, gait, tone, posture, movement and function without a documented peripheral explanation.

The first specialist information will soon make some parts of that problem much more determinate. But Movement I ends before those determinations are allowed to organise the earlier record. At the specialist threshold, the feet have not become a minor appendix to an already solved neurological case. Nor has Parkinsonism yet become an inherited diagnosis through which every earlier fact is automatically interpreted. The GP is escalating because the presentation is getting harder to understand and harder to manage. The patient is losing function. The feet remain painful and visibly abnormal. The movement abnormalities are spreading beyond them. What remains missing is not data in general. It is the account of how these facts belong together.

That unresolved relationship is the object crossing the hospital door. The clinical material does not arrive as Parkinson's disease accompanied by an established second diagnosis. It arrives as a person with bilateral painful and structurally abnormal feet, an unusual and worsening gait, toe clawing, increased tone, brisk reflexes, impaired initiation, declining dexterity and growing difficulty functioning at work. Podiatry has a real task. Neurology now has one too. The fact that both can be true is not yet an explanation of their relationship. At the point of referral, that relationship still has to be investigated. The record has not yet supplied the proposition that will later organise it.

## II. The Neuropathy Object Is Made

The specialist examination does not begin by confirming a second condition in the feet. It begins by making the neurological problem more definite. The gait is described as “strikingly abnormal.” There is marked rigidity and bradykinesia, little tremor, increased tone and brisk reflexes, with the overall picture recognised as severe young-onset Parkinsonism. That is an important completion, but it is narrower than the whole presentation that arrived. It answers whether a serious Parkinsonian syndrome is present. It does not yet answer how the painful, structurally abnormal feet belong inside that syndrome. The feet remain in the room after Parkinsonism becomes intelligible.

One lower-limb observation makes that unresolved relation especially difficult to ignore. The patient walks with an appearance resembling foot drop, yet the neurologist writes that it is “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP.” The wording is observational and discriminating rather than diagnostic. It says what the movement resembles and what it does not appear to be. It does not establish dystonia as the explanation of the foot pain. But it places an abnormal lower-limb posture squarely inside the neurological examination before any peripheral-neuropathy explanation is visible. From this point onward, a later separation of the feet requires work that this observation does not itself perform.

Structural pathology is also preserved in the same specialist assessment. Pes planus and hammertoes are recorded. Those findings are compatible with what primary care had already seen and with the Podiatry pathway already under way. Their presence strengthens the case for genuine local foot disease, not for a fictional or purely neurological foot problem. Yet again the relation remains unsettled. A structural lesion can be painful without being neurologically independent. An abnormal posture can alter loading without explaining every lesion. The examination establishes coexistence more readily than separation. The patient now has both a serious movement disorder and objectively abnormal feet. The difficult question is how much explanatory distance should be placed between them.

The specialist history then reaches backwards. It records that corns and callouses, fallen arches and foot problems had existed for years, and that leaving bar work in 2012 reduced the pain in the feet to a degree described as “out of the picture,” while the leg tension continued. This is clinically meaningful history. It supplies a temporal difference between two experiences. But the carrier matters. The surviving evidence is a specialist-authored record of what the patient was understood to report retrospectively. It does not give us an independent 2012 statement. It can support the proposition that different symptoms followed different courses. It cannot, by itself, establish biological independence.

The record goes further and says that the patient began to realise that the foot pain and the leg tension were “not the same problem.” Again, there is a real distinction here. Pain becoming less prominent while tension remains can legitimately lead a person to experience them as different phenomena. But “different” is still not “unrelated.” A patient can distinguish sensations, timings or burdens without making a mechanistic claim about their causes. That difference becomes crucial because the completed specialist product will later attribute a much stronger separation to him. The evidential task is to follow the transition without pretending that the patient's experiential distinction already contains the clinician's later explanatory architecture.

The word “burning” appears when the completed specialist synthesis turns to the leg and foot symptoms. In the disclosed pre-specialist record, the surviving vocabulary includes bilateral pain, corns, callouses, pressure areas, toe clawing and muscle tension. I do not locate the same descriptor there. That does not establish that “burning” was absent from the patient's experience or from an unrecorded bedside history. It establishes something more limited: its earliest located documentary appearance in this 2017 chain is inside the specialist product that is also constructing a neuropathy possibility. The word may therefore be patient report, elicited history or specialist compression. The available carrier does not presently let us choose confidently among them.

That uncertainty matters because “burning” can perform evidential work. It is a symptom quality commonly capable of entering neuropathic reasoning, whereas “pain in the feet” is less specific. That sequence cannot safely be reversed, with the hypothesis treated as proof of the descriptor's provenance. The safer reconstruction is that the completed product contains a symptom description more suggestive of a sensory mechanism than the earlier surviving records do. Whether that refinement came from a more detailed history, a bedside conversation or the clinician's own synthesis is unresolved. What can be seen is that the language has become more diagnostically organised before the record shows a correspondingly explicit neuropathy-directed examination.

The next phrase changes the architecture more visibly. The leg symptoms, including corns, callouses and burning, “may have been completely separate.” The modal phrase preserves uncertainty, but “completely separate” introduces a strong relation. It does not merely describe two experiences as different. It makes full explanatory independence available as a possibility. That possibility might be clinically reasonable in a patient with real structural disease and symptoms that had varied over time. The question is what work earned the word “completely.” The visible record contains coexistence, temporal divergence and structural pathology. It does not yet show a documented comparison demonstrating that the painful feet were independent of the dystonic-looking lower-limb phenomenon.

The sentence then offers an alternative: “perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy.” This is the first located point at which peripheral neuropathy becomes an explicit explanatory object in the disclosed 2017 chain. The grammar is cautious twice over. “Perhaps” marks hypothesis; “secondary” already imagines a mechanism requiring some cause beyond the Parkinsonian syndrome itself. Nothing in the wording claims confirmation. The clinical question is therefore not whether the neurologist was entitled to consider neuropathy. The record plainly contains enough complexity for multiple mechanisms to be conceivable. The narrower inquiry is what examination, sensory history or investigation made this particular mechanism sufficiently grounded to enter the authoritative specialist account.

Some evidence can be identified. There is longstanding foot pain. The completed history now contains “burning.” There is significant historical alcohol use. Broad blood investigations were undertaken, including B12, folate and serum electrophoresis, and the neurological work-up was substantial. Yet the disclosed corpus does not show nerve-conduction studies, EMG, a mapped sensory-loss pattern or another objective test confirming peripheral neuropathy at this stage. That absence does not prove such work never occurred. It defines what the surviving authoritative record can demonstrate. The general neurological investigation became sophisticated very quickly. The visible investigation of the specific feet–movement relation did not deepen at the same rate.

Alcohol then changes documentary function. Earlier, it entered the record because the patient used it to relax muscles and because his intake was clinically significant. In the specialist synthesis it becomes something more: the possible neuropathy is said to be perhaps related “in some respect” to the significant original alcohol intake. This is a legitimate clinical inference to consider, not a fact already contained in the alcohol history itself. The transition therefore needs to remain visible. Historical exposure has become etiological support. The sentence preserves uncertainty, but it also gives peripheral neuropathy a candidate cause, allowing the emerging object to acquire an internal structure: symptoms, mechanism, and possible aetiology.

Immediately after that causal possibility comes “Not clear as this has improved.” The phrase is easy to read as a brake on overconfidence, and it is one. The clinician does not present the peripheral explanation as settled. But “Not clear” also has another possible documentary effect: it can allow an unresolved hypothesis to remain in the completed account without requiring the uncertainty to be resolved before the document travels. The relevant scale also needs care. The historical improvement referred to the foot pain becoming less prominent after bar work ended; corns and callouses remained, and the larger neurological problem continued to develop. Relative improvement in one symptom cannot silently become resolution of the foot problem as a whole.

The strongest attribution to the patient follows. The document says that he had, “in his own mind,” “markedly divorced” the feelings of tension in the legs from the corns, callouses and pains in the feet. This sentence contains two layers at once. The document attributes a distinction to the patient, but the formulation “markedly divorced” is the clinician's textual characterisation unless another source shows the patient using those exact words. The earlier history supports some patient-perceived difference between pain and tension because they followed different courses. It does not independently establish that the patient understood the feet as neurologically unrelated to the tension or Parkinsonian syndrome.

The wording matters because attribution can change the apparent source of an explanatory relation. If a clinician writes that two phenomena “may have been completely separate,” the reader can recognise a medical hypothesis. If the same document then says the patient had “markedly divorced” them in his own mind, separation can begin to look jointly authored by clinician and patient. That may be an accurate representation of the consultation. The disclosed corpus cannot independently verify the exact discussion. The safe conclusion is narrower: the completed specialist document records the patient as having made a strong distinction. It proves the attribution existed by completion; it does not independently prove the patient's exact formulation.

This is also the point at which the asymmetry with the neurological examination becomes sharpest. The completed product has developed a route from foot symptoms to possible peripheral neuropathy, and from neuropathy to possible alcohol contribution. It has also developed a patient-attributed route toward separation. By contrast, the earlier observed lower-limb posture “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture” has not, in the visible material, generated an equivalent feet–dystonia inquiry. The source does not show whether that comparison was considered and rejected orally or simply not recorded. Function must come before motive. What is visible is that one explanatory route becomes elaborated in the final text while the other remains primarily an examination observation.

That difference does not by itself show error. There are benign explanations. The clinician may have judged the dystonic-looking gait to be part of the Parkinsonian syndrome while regarding the pain and structural lesions as a separate problem requiring a different explanation. The patient's retrospective history of symptom divergence may have reinforced that view. Alcohol may reasonably have raised concern about peripheral nerve disease. Structural foot pathology may have made multiple mechanisms more likely. A complete record might reveal additional sensory examination not reproduced here. Those possibilities remain live. The point at this stage is not that the peripheral hypothesis was illegitimate, but that its documentary organisation is becoming more visible than the clinical work specifically establishing it.

The distinction between investigation and explanation can now be stated without resolving the case. The investigation has unquestionably become more sophisticated with respect to Parkinsonism: detailed neurological examination, laboratory testing, imaging and specialist differential reasoning are under way. Structural foot disease is also being taken seriously. But the explanation of the feet is developing in another direction at the same time: burning, possible complete separation, possible secondary peripheral neuropathy, possible alcohol causation, and strong patient-attributed differentiation. Those are increasingly organised propositions about how the feet belong in the case. The visible record does not show the feet–movement relation receiving an equally organised investigation before those propositions are assembled.

The final-document chronology makes the reconstruction harder. The preserved specialist product is a completed synthesis, not a timestamped transcript of bedside reasoning. Later within the document, a DaTscan result was added at transcription finalisation on 13 July 2017, proving that the document remained open to new material after the earlier clinical encounter. What that timestamp does not tell us is when “burning,” “completely separate,” “perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy,” the alcohol linkage or “markedly divorced” were first written or first thought. For those phrases, the production date remains unknown. Their order in the finished document cannot safely be treated as the sequence in which the clinical ideas arose.

By the end of this first specialist synthesis, the neuropathy object exists. It does not exist as a confirmed diagnosis. It exists as a conditional explanatory construction assembled from real foot disease, retrospective symptom history, a newly visible “burning” descriptor, significant alcohol exposure and a proposed separation from the dominant leg tension. The construction remains explicitly uncertain. Yet it has already done important work: it has made a peripheral route intelligible enough to enter the completed specialist product. The unresolved question is no longer simply what is happening in the feet. It now includes a second-order question that will follow the object forward: what evidence earned this particular separation enough authority to travel?

## III. The Object Travels

The neuropathy object does not leave the specialist document in the same form in which it first appeared. Before it travels longitudinally, it is given a higher-order role. In the genetic reasoning, PARK2 is considered because of the patient's very young age, akinetic-rigid presentation, relative symmetry and lack of a dominant family history. Alongside that reasoning appear rarer monogenic possibilities, including PARK6, PARK7 and “even PARK 9 (the neuropathy question).” The local foot hypothesis has now entered the architecture of the Parkinsonian differential. Nothing in that transition, by itself, confirms neuropathy. What changes is its conceptual position inside the case.

That movement matters because propositions can gain status without gaining new evidence. “Perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy” is a local explanatory possibility. “PARK 9 (the neuropathy question)” treats neuropathy as a phenotypic clue capable of bearing on the cause of the neurological syndrome itself. The genetic reasoning may have been entirely legitimate as differential diagnosis. The evidential dependency nevertheless remains: if neuropathy is uncertain, any inference using its presence must inherit some of that uncertainty. The finished document does not need to be wrong for this to matter. It needs only to make a conditional premise useful enough to participate in a more sophisticated explanatory structure.

The route upward also creates a possible feedback effect. Once neuropathy appears inside specialist genetic reasoning, a later reader may encounter it not merely as a tentative foot hypothesis but as something important enough to influence consideration of rare Parkinsonian disorders. That placement can make the proposition feel clinically weightier than its original evidential basis alone would suggest. The record does not establish that any clinician consciously reasoned backwards from PARK9 to greater confidence in neuropathy. Such recursive validation would be an inference. What the document does establish is structural: the neuropathy question now appears at more than one explanatory level inside the same authoritative specialist product.

This is where documentary completion begins to matter in its own right. A bedside thought is transient. A completed specialist letter is portable. It can be copied, read later, summarised, cited, or treated as background by clinicians who were not present when the underlying history was elicited. The July product therefore becomes more than a record of one consultation. It becomes a carrier. That does not mean every later clinician adopted every proposition inside it, or even read every line. It means that the information state now has a durable form through which a conditional neuropathy possibility can remain available after the encounter that produced it has ended.

The document itself was not produced in one timeless instant. An addendum at transcription finalisation on 13 July records the completed DaTscan from earlier that day. The final letter therefore contains material generated across more than one clinical moment. This matters because the finished page can visually compress those moments into a single coherent object. The reader sees examination, history, interpretation, differential diagnosis and later-arriving scan result together. The source can reliably show what the completed document contained. It cannot, without version history, show when every sentence first existed. Documentary unity should not be mistaken for simultaneous production.

The neuropathy paragraph sits inside that completed unity with a particular advantage: it carries its own uncertainty forward. “May have been.” “Perhaps.” “Not clear.” Those words protect against claiming a certainty the clinician did not express. Yet uncertainty does not prevent inheritance. A later reader does not need neuropathy to be confirmed before treating it as a relevant possibility already considered by specialist Neurology. This is the first longitudinal property of the object. Its conditionality can survive intact while its availability increases. A proposition can remain grammatically provisional and still become easier to reuse because a respected source has already made it clinically intelligible.

The first question for every later appearance must therefore be genealogical. Is the later neuropathy reference another evidential root, or a branch growing from this one? A fresh sensory examination demonstrating neuropathy would be a new root. Neurophysiology would be a new root. A newly observed distribution of symptoms might be one. A later note that simply calls the pain neuropathic may not be. Continuity of vocabulary can look like continuity of verification when the sources are read quickly. That effect has to be resisted. Repetition proves that a proposition remained in circulation. It does not, by itself, show that its original evidential foundation became deeper.

This distinction is particularly important because the case does not become clinically inactive after 2017. Treatment changes, symptoms change, and the feet continue to generate observations. The danger would be to interpret all later activity as evidence that the original neuropathy object was repeatedly reconfirmed. Clinical action can occur under uncertainty. A treatment may be chosen because it is reasonable for a symptom phenotype, not because the aetiology has been proved. A clinician may inherit a differential and keep it open while exploring other mechanisms. The later record therefore has to be read event by event: what new evidence appeared, and what part of the inherited explanation did that evidence actually earn?

One early event is unusually useful because it occurs close to the founding period. In 2018, a note looking back to a two-week procyclidine withdrawal in September or October 2017 records that the first thing to change rapidly was the return of the problem around the outside-left-foot corn area. Symptoms worsened, and the patient became more “shifty” and “unrelaxed” off procyclidine before restarting treatment. This is not proof that the corn itself was neurological, nor that the historical bilateral pain was dystonic. It is evidence that a focal foot territory changed with neurological treatment state. The inherited separation has encountered its first documented boundary-crossing observation.

The scale of that observation must remain exact. A treatment-state change can establish relation without establishing cause. The return of a painful or troublesome foot territory during procyclidine withdrawal does not tell us whether the mechanism was dystonia, altered posture, altered loading, changed sensory experience, or something else. It does make a fully independent account of that territory less simple than it would otherwise be. The correct question is not whether procyclidine disproved neuropathy. It plainly did not. The question is whether this observation should have altered how confidently the foot problem could continue to be organised as separate from the neurological state.

The surviving record shows adaptation more readily than reconstruction. Procyclidine is resumed because the broader symptoms worsened. What is less visible is an explicit return to the founding relation: if neurological treatment alters the foot territory, should the earlier separation itself now be reconsidered? The absence of such a sentence cannot establish that no clinician thought about it. It does reveal what the travelling record makes available to a later reviewer. The local treatment problem is handled. The deeper explanatory architecture is not visibly rebuilt around this observation. That difference between responding to a new fact and reconstructing an inherited model will recur throughout the history.

The same period also strengthens the counter-case. Structural disease does not vanish because a neurological medication changes one aspect of the foot experience. Corns and callouses remain physically present. Podiatry treatment remains clinically intelligible. Mixed mechanisms become more, not less, plausible. That is why the manuscript must not force every boundary-crossing event into a single-cause story. The pressure on neuropathy is not that another mechanism appears and therefore replaces it. The pressure is whether evidence increasingly demonstrates that the feet cannot be cleanly allocated outside the neurological presentation without qualification. A genuinely mixed model can be more accurate than either a purely peripheral or purely neurological one.

As the record moves forward, the object also begins to change linguistically. Later clinicians can speak of “neuropathic” pain or use medications conventionally associated with neuropathic pain without thereby recreating the 2017 evidential pathway from first principles. The exact source and wording matter each time. “Neuropathic pain” may describe a pain quality, a treatment category, a working diagnosis or inherited language. “Peripheral neuropathy” is a more specific mechanistic proposition. “Alcohol-related neuropathy” adds aetiology. These terms overlap, but they are not interchangeable. The genealogy must therefore preserve not only whether neuropathy appears, but what version of the object appears and what work supports it on that occasion.

This is also where the patient's later orbit begins, although it is still too early to describe it fully. Once neuropathy exists in the specialist record, subsequent foot experiences occur in a clinical world where that possibility is already available. A later painful episode does not begin from the same information state as the April 2017 complaint. The explanatory field has changed. That does not prove the patient was explicitly told “this is neuropathy” at every stage, nor that every later clinician relied consciously on the 2017 sentence. The documentary point is simpler: future interpretation no longer starts from an empty space. The object is now part of the inherited clinical vocabulary.

The question of discussion with the patient remains unresolved. The completed document is patient-facing and records the neuropathy possibility, but the disclosed contemporaneous material does not independently demonstrate when, how, or whether the phrase “peripheral neuropathy” was discussed orally during the admission. A final letter can reliably establish that the proposition existed by completion. It cannot automatically establish bedside chronology. The distinction is particularly important because later experience may depend on what the patient understood at the time, while the documentary genealogy depends on what later readers could inherit. Those are related histories, but they are not the same history and should not be fused.

By the time the object begins to travel, then, its foundation has three distinct layers. There are direct observations: painful and structurally abnormal feet, Parkinsonian signs, and an abnormal lower-limb posture with dystonic appearance. There is patient-attributed history: symptom divergence and longstanding foot problems. And there is specialist synthesis: possible separation, possible peripheral neuropathy, possible alcohol contribution, and later genetic use of “the neuropathy question.” The distinction among these layers is more important than any single phrase. If later records inherit the synthesis without reconstructing its inputs, those layers can become harder to see separately even while every individual sentence remains technically cautious.

The standing question therefore changes slightly as the object moves beyond its point of origin. It is no longer only: what clinical work earned peripheral neuropathy a place in the record? It becomes: what new work, if any, earned its continued place each time it reappeared? A proposition can survive for legitimate reasons. It can remain plausible. It can coexist with new mechanisms. It can be useful for treatment without being proved. But every later use has an evidential cost. If the object continues to organise the feet, the record should allow us to ask whether that authority came from fresh observation, inherited background, therapeutic pragmatism, or simple documentary continuity.

At this stage the question is still not whether neuropathy was right or wrong. The more immediate fact is that it has become portable. A conditional proposition generated inside one specialist synthesis has acquired a place in the completed record, a role in higher-order genetic reasoning, and enough durability to remain available when later foot phenomena emerge. The first later neurological treatment-state observation already complicates any simple separation without abolishing the peripheral possibility. That is how the next phase begins: not with disappearance, and not with confirmation, but with an inherited explanatory object encountering evidence that will repeatedly cross the boundary it helped to draw.

## IV. Pressure Without Disappearance

By 2020, the feet produce another observation that does not fit neatly inside a stable peripheral box. The left lower limb again appears to throw outward in a way that resembles foot drop. This time the examining neurologist tests the relevant power directly: dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, inversion and eversion are normal. The movement is then described as “actually parkinsonian.” The wording matters because the clinical work is visible. A misleading appearance is examined against preserved strength, and the conclusion changes accordingly. This does not explain the patient's foot pain. It does show that a foot-level phenomenon can belong directly to the Parkinsonian disorder.

That observation should not be allowed to do more than it earned. Normal power and a Parkinsonian movement do not disprove peripheral neuropathy. Peripheral nerve disease can coexist with Parkinsonism, structural pathology and abnormal movement. Nor does one Parkinsonian foot phenomenon retrospectively convert every earlier corn, callus or pain into dystonia. The significance is narrower but still important. In 2017, the lower limb had already looked dystonic. In 2020, another apparently peripheral-looking foot movement is actively tested and brought inside the neurological model. The inherited separation therefore has to coexist with increasing evidence that the neurological disorder reaches into the same anatomical territory.

The same 2020 material records excessive movement or urge in the feet and striking sensitivity. These findings do not line up into a single mechanism automatically. Sensitivity can pull the reader toward a peripheral sensory interpretation; abnormal movement can pull in another direction. The point is not to decide which should win. It is to notice that the feet are becoming a site where several clinical languages overlap. The neuropathy object survives because the new evidence does not exclude it. But survival is not the same as confirmation. Each additional mechanism makes the question of relation more important: which component explains which part of the patient's experience, and how is that distinction being tested?

In 2022, the feet acquire a temporal pattern. They can take twenty to twenty-five minutes to “warm up” in the morning. The patient continues to describe soreness around corns and callus, at times using a “coronal” description around the edges of the feet, and wonders whether part of the experience may be neuralgic. Cramping, however, is almost absent. The record does not turn that morning pattern into a definitive mechanism. It adds another kind of information: symptoms vary with time and movement state. The neuropathy object can accommodate pain and sensitivity, but temporal responsiveness also keeps a neurological relation available.

This is where the distinction between symptom description and explanatory inheritance becomes increasingly important. A patient wondering whether pain is “neuralgic” is not the same evidential event as a clinician demonstrating peripheral neuropathy. A clinician later using neuropathic terminology is not necessarily adding a new root either. Every sensory word cannot therefore be treated as another increment of confirmation. By 2022 the case contains persistent structural pain, abnormal movement, morning state change and a longstanding peripheral possibility. The explanatory field is widening. The crucial question is not whether more words for pain are available. It is whether the relation among those phenomena is being reconstructed.

In 2023, the scale of the suffering becomes impossible to reduce to a technical descriptor. The GP records severe bilateral burning pain affecting the feet and lower legs and escalates pregabalin. The suffering is described at a level that is psychologically overwhelming, including language indicating a wish for death during extreme pain. At the same time, the treatment begins to help. Those facts must remain together. “Improved on pregabalin” cannot be allowed to erase the baseline from which improvement occurred. A response can be real and clinically important while the remaining condition is still intolerable, disabling or dominant. Relative improvement is not resolution.

Pregabalin therefore enters the genealogy carefully. Benefit from a drug commonly used for neuropathic pain can support the practical reasonableness of treating a neuropathic component. It cannot, by itself, establish peripheral neuropathy as the cause of the feet. Analgesic response does not function like a biopsy. Nor does a treatment need etiological certainty to be appropriate. The evidential value is more modest: some component of the pain was modifiable by pregabalin. The explanatory danger comes only if that benefit is allowed to travel as retrospective validation of the original peripheral hypothesis. Treatment response and mechanism remain separate propositions unless further clinical work connects them.

At this point another distinction becomes unavoidable. Explaining pain and controlling pain are not the same clinical task. The record is active: pregabalin is escalated, Parkinson medication is adjusted, OFF phenomena are considered, and structural foot care continues. Yet the patient's suffering remains severe enough to dominate function and psychological endurance. If neuropathy were the right explanation, the pain still required control. If dystonia were the right explanation, the pain still required control. If the answer were mixed, that changed nothing about the immediate burden. Diagnostic uncertainty may explain caution about mechanism; it does not by itself answer what was being done to bring the pain within a tolerable scale.

A succession of partially effective treatments can create its own documentary impression of progress. Pregabalin may reduce one component. Dopaminergic adjustment may alter another. Podiatry may address a focal mechanical lesion. Each intervention can be reasonable and each response real. But therapeutic activity is not the same as therapeutic control. The outcome has to be measured against what remains: the ability to walk, work, sleep, wear shoes, stand, leave the house, and endure the first part of the day. Otherwise the same structural error can recur at treatment level: the fact that something helped begins to stand in for the much larger question of whether the patient's pain was actually brought under meaningful control.

At the same time, the Parkinson nurse records a different relation. Severe foot pain is accompanied by reduced mobility and shuffling, and symptoms worsen in the morning or later in the day when Sinemet is absent or has worn off. The nurse's judgement is that the patient is undertreated and that the leg pain and shuffling are OFF-related. A medication change follows, with Stalevo introduced. This is a fresh clinical judgement tied to observed or reported movement-state dependence. It does not erase the neuropathic language. Instead, another explanatory branch is added: at least some lower-limb pain and mobility difficulty appear related to dopaminergic state.

The detailed neurological review develops the same pressure further. Lower-limb cramping raises the possibility of Parkinsonian dystonia, especially OFF dystonia. The timing of pain and walking also matters: the first walk can be difficult, a second can be much better, and later evening deterioration can return. These observations do not prove that the historical foot pain was dystonic. They establish that the treating record now contains an explicit neurological mechanism capable of producing lower-limb discomfort in the same patient. The original peripheral possibility has not disappeared, but it no longer occupies the field alone. The question becomes whether the model is being reconstructed or simply enlarged.

The clinician also voices a challenge to the peripheral account. Peripheral neuropathy, he notes, would not ordinarily appear “literally overnight,” notwithstanding the long history of foot problems. That sentence is important because it shows active resistance to a simple neuropathy explanation rather than passive inheritance. Yet the same review does not abandon peripheral neuropathy. Instead, it describes a “misalignment”: the patient understands the feet as separate, while the clinician believes much of the problem may be explainable through Parkinson's disease. The old separation has therefore become contested inside the consultation itself. What was once attributed to the patient's own distinction is now recognised as a point of disagreement.

That reversal complicates any claim that the service simply preserved a single fixed account unchanged. The record shows genuine reconsideration. A Parkinsonian contribution to pain is entertained. OFF dystonia is considered. The patient's separation is questioned. Treatment is adjusted around dopaminergic state. These are meaningful changes. But reconsideration at the level of individual symptoms is not automatically the same as reconstruction of the founding architecture. The harder question is whether the record ever explicitly returns to the earlier proposition—feet as possibly separate and possibly neuropathic—and states that accumulated evidence has reduced its organising authority. Local openness is visible. Global reclassification is harder to locate.

The Fabry investigation belongs inside that distinction. Because the case combines Parkinsonism with a continuing peripheral-neuropathy question, alpha-galactosidase and related testing are ordered to explore an overlap disorder. This is not evidence of diagnostic inertia. It is active investigation generated partly by the persistence of the neuropathy object. The later normal result removes one proposed overlap route but does not test peripheral neuropathy as a whole. The episode shows the object exercising clinical efficacy while remaining uncertain: neuropathy is important enough to shape what rare alternative is investigated, even though the proposition itself has not been firmly demonstrated. Conditionality and influence continue to coexist.

The same period makes mixed mechanisms increasingly plausible. Pain can respond to pregabalin. Lower-limb symptoms can vary with dopaminergic state. Cramping can look like OFF dystonia. Structural corns and callouses remain physically present. None of these observations has to cancel the others. A clinically sophisticated model may need more than one mechanism. But mixed models create their own evidential burden. If every new observation is accommodated by adding another branch, it becomes difficult to tell what evidence would actually reduce the authority of an older branch. The question is not whether mixture is suspicious. It is whether the model has an identifiable way to become less committed to one of its inherited components.

By 2024, the record describes substantial pain relief from pregabalin, particularly on the right. That description needs its scale restored. The patient still describes his life as “a lot smaller,” and standing activities such as darts can worsen the feet. The original scale therefore matters. A later summary saying Lyrica helped would be true but incomplete if it implied recovery. The patient remains treatment-dependent and functionally constrained. More importantly, another foot phenomenon survives the analgesic benefit: toe curling persists even when the pain is greatly reduced. Pain and abnormal posture no longer travel together perfectly. The treatment has separated two symptom dimensions without establishing the mechanism of either one.

That divergence is clinically useful. If pregabalin reduces pain while toe curling persists, the record has evidence that the foot problem is not a single undifferentiated symptom. It may contain sensory pain, structural provocation, abnormal movement, or other components with different treatment responsiveness. This weakens any attempt to use one medication response as proof of the whole explanatory model. It also complicates a purely neurological account if pain can be independently modulated. The appropriate conclusion is not that neuropathy wins or loses. The appropriate conclusion is that the feet require finer differentiation than the founding binary of separate peripheral problem versus Parkinsonian problem could easily provide.

The patient's description of medication as “spot on” likewise has to remain on the correct scale. It records meaningful therapeutic benefit in a particular domain. It does not mean the overall foot problem is solved, that function has returned to normal, or that the underlying explanation has been confirmed. The surviving account still includes restricted life, provocation by standing and persistent toe curling. This is exactly how documentary compression can distort without containing a false sentence. “Lyrica worked” may be accurate. If detached from what remained, it can sound like a larger completion than the patient's actual condition supports. Baseline, response and residual burden have to remain in the same frame.

The accumulated record now places pressure on the original separation from several directions. A foot movement has been judged Parkinsonian. Pain and shuffling have been assessed as OFF-related. OFF dystonia has entered the neurological differential. Dopaminergic state changes the lower-limb experience. Pregabalin modifies pain without resolving toe curling. Structural pathology persists. The patient and clinician disagree over how separate the feet really are. None of those facts excludes peripheral neuropathy. Together, however, they make a single stable allocation increasingly difficult. The object survives not because later evidence confirms it straightforwardly, but because a mixed model can continue to contain it while other mechanisms accumulate around it.

That is the central distinction of this phase. The service is not clinically static. Treatments change, hypotheses broaden, rare overlap disease is investigated, and Parkinsonian contributions to the feet are explicitly considered. The record therefore cannot support a simple claim that nothing was reconsidered. What remains less visible is a global documentary sentence saying that the original peripheral separation itself has lost authority because later evidence repeatedly crosses it. The difference matters. Adaptation responds to new facts. Reconstruction changes the organising relation among old and new facts. The surviving notes show a great deal of the first. Whether they show enough of the second remains an open question.

By the end of 2024, the neuropathy object is under pressure but has not disappeared. It remains clinically available because pain retains sensory qualities, pregabalin is useful, and overlap investigation has continued. At the same time, the feet have become increasingly entangled with Parkinsonian state, abnormal movement and dystonic possibility. The patient is not living inside a completed explanation. He is living inside a set of partially overlapping explanations, each capable of accounting for some part of the experience and none yet visibly reorganising the whole. The next movement begins when that unresolvedness stops being merely clinical and becomes something the patient himself starts to challenge explicitly.

## V. The Patient Challenges the Field

By early 2025, the feet are again the patient's main concern. The language is not mild. He says, “my feet disturb me.” He hobbles on getting out of bed. The feet can be too sore to tie his runners. He describes the pain as “murder in the feet,” and the first roughly 150 footsteps of the morning as “torture.” Those descriptions belong beside, not beneath, the earlier treatment-response language. Pregabalin had helped. That remains true. It had not converted the problem into a minor residual complaint. The surviving scale is still one of major suffering, functional limitation and daily anticipation of pain.

By now the case carries two longitudinal questions that cannot be allowed to collapse into one another. What mechanism explains the feet? And what has actually happened to the pain while that mechanism remains disputed? The first can remain medically uncertain for years. The second cannot be answered by naming neuropathy, dystonia, structural disease, or a mixture of all three. Whatever the label, the patient is still waking into severe pain despite years of clinical contact and several treatment routes. The record can therefore be busy while the central therapeutic fact remains stark: explanation has multiplied more successfully than relief.

A second observation now carries more explanatory pressure: once the Parkinson medication reaches his system, the feet improve. The treating neurologist acknowledges that relation. The response is not interpreted as proof that Parkinson's disease is the sole cause of the pain. Instead, the explanation remains broader: levodopa can alter the experience of pain whatever its underlying source. That is an important clinical caution. Treatment response does not identify mechanism automatically. But it also means that the feet are now repeatedly demonstrating dependence on neurological treatment state. The inherited peripheral possibility has to coexist with an increasingly visible dopaminergic relation that is no longer merely retrospective.

The clinician's wording keeps neuropathy alive explicitly: “If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here…” The conditional grammar is striking because of how long it has persisted. The proposition is still not presented as firmly demonstrated. Yet it remains available enough to organise treatment reasoning and to coexist with a dopaminergic explanation of pain experience. This is not necessarily contradiction. A mixed model may be exactly what the clinical picture requires. The evidential question is different: what, after years of boundary-crossing observations, would count as enough contrary or reorganising evidence to reduce the authority of the peripheral component rather than simply add another mechanism beside it?

The proposed use of Sinemet CR at night sharpens that question. Part of the rationale is to improve the morning state, including the painful foot experience that is worst before medication takes effect. The intervention therefore treats the feet through the Parkinsonian medication schedule without first requiring the whole pain mechanism to be classified as Parkinsonian. Clinically, that can be pragmatic and appropriate. Documentary function is another matter. A treatment can operate across diagnostic uncertainty while the underlying explanatory object remains unresolved. The patient's lived experience is therefore beginning to diverge from the apparent stability of the language: the treatment model is changing around the feet faster than the founding peripheral proposition is disappearing.

What happens next cannot be understood only as a medication event. From the patient's perspective, the Sinemet and Sinemet CR experience changes what kind of question he is asking. For years, the feet had been painful, structurally abnormal, variably responsive to pregabalin and increasingly entangled with movement state. The new observation does not prove that every historical symptom was neurological. It does give him a concrete experiential reason to doubt that the old separation adequately captures the problem. The shift is from symptom management toward explanatory challenge. He is no longer asking only how to obtain relief. He is beginning to ask how the feet came to be understood in the way they were.

That distinction matters because the patient's later complaint is not an independent reconstruction of the 2017 documentary process. It is the culmination of living through the case. By 19 March 2025, he alleges that an early neuropathy and alcohol-related framing became fixed and was insufficiently reassessed despite later changes in pain, function and neurological evidence. He alleges that a fresh neurological account of the feet was not adequately pursued. The complaint is strong evidence of what he had come to believe after eight years. It is not proof of how the original specialist sentences were generated, nor proof that every later clinician inherited them in the same way.

The complaint's evidential role becomes clearer when its requests are considered. The patient does not ask only for a change in medication. He asks for independent review, full disclosure, a written explanation and preservation of records. Those requests imply that the problem, from his perspective, has become historical and documentary as well as symptomatic. He wants to know not merely what the current diagnosis should be, but how the explanatory model was formed and maintained. That is why the complaint matters to this manuscript. It marks the point at which lived unresolvedness and documentary suspicion converge, even though neither can yet prove the mechanism of the other.

The allegation of anchoring must remain exactly that: an allegation. The record supports the existence of an early conditional neuropathy proposition and later continued use of neuropathic and peripheral language. It supports repeated neurological observations capable of putting pressure on simple separation. It does not, by itself, establish that any clinician deliberately refused to reconsider the feet or consciously preserved an explanation in the face of known contradiction. Intention would require different evidence. Function comes first. What did the early wording make available? What later work relied on it? What changed around it? What remained conditional? Those questions can be answered more safely than why any individual thought or wrote as they did.

The complaint also retrospectively names the alcohol issue more sharply than the early record did. From the patient's perspective, alcohol had become part of an explanatory frame that attached the painful feet to possible peripheral neuropathy. That interpretation is understandable when the 2017 specialist wording is read alongside later notes. But the complaint cannot establish exactly how much causal weight alcohol carried in every clinical decision over the intervening years. The genealogy must therefore remain granular. Alcohol was real history. Its neuropathic significance was proposed. The proposition remained conditional. Later records continued to consider alcohol-related neuropathy as a possibility. Those are separate facts that should not be collapsed into one continuous proven causal diagnosis.

The patient's challenge is strongest where it meets independently visible later evidence. A procyclidine-state change had affected the outside-left-foot territory. A later foot movement had been judged Parkinsonian. OFF dystonia entered the treating record. Leg pain and shuffling were described as OFF-related. Morning symptoms changed with dopaminergic state. Sinemet was now accepted to alter the foot-pain experience. None of those observations proves the patient's retrospective thesis. Together, they make his demand for explanatory reconstruction intelligible. He is not challenging an explanation in a vacuum. He is pointing to a history in which the boundary between neurological disease and the feet has repeatedly been crossed.

The complaint nevertheless risks a retrospective simplification of its own. Once a patient has experienced dopaminergic relief, it is tempting to reinterpret every earlier painful event as evidence that the original peripheral account was mistaken. The record does not support that certainty. Structural pathology remained genuine. Pregabalin relieved some pain. Toe curling and pain could respond differently. A peripheral component was never definitively excluded. The stronger patient-level claim is therefore not “the feet were always Parkinsonian.” It is that the longstanding separation had become, in his experience, increasingly difficult to reconcile with what later treatment and movement-state changes were showing. That claim is more limited, and more firmly grounded.

The treating neurologist's response of 25 March preserves a different balance. Parkinson's disease, he says in substance, had always been central. Peripheral neuropathy was a risk, worry or reasonable possibility rather than a definitive diagnosis. He emphasises that Parkinson's disease can magnify pain whatever its cause and acknowledges that Sinemet helps the patient's experience of the feet. He also remains open to there being “a degree of peripheral neuropathy here.” This is not a defence of a confirmed neuropathy diagnosis. It is a defence of maintaining multiple possibilities. The disagreement is therefore not simply fact versus error. It concerns how much organising authority a long-standing possibility was entitled to retain.

That response also provides counter-evidence against the strongest version of the patient's allegation. The neurologist points to Fabry investigation and to continued willingness to consider other causes. The record independently shows OFF dystonia being considered, dopaminergic undertreatment being addressed and later treatment adjusted. These are signs of adaptation. The service was not operating with a single frozen explanation incapable of change. The more difficult question survives because adaptation can occur without a complete reworking of the founding relation. The issue is not whether clinicians ever considered alternatives. They did. It is whether accumulating alternatives ever caused the original separation itself to lose documentary or clinical authority.

The phrase “If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here” is especially revealing in that respect. It demonstrates uncertainty rather than dogma. Yet by 2025 the object has survived for approximately eight years without becoming either clearly confirmed or clearly retired. That longevity may reflect appropriate caution in a genuinely mixed presentation. It may also mean that the proposition had no clearly articulated exit condition. The distinction cannot be decided by grammar alone. A cautious sentence can be highly corrigible if contrary evidence would cause it to recede. The same cautious sentence can persist indefinitely if every new observation is simply added beside it. The longitudinal question is what the record shows happening in practice.

The patient, meanwhile, lives on the scale of the feet rather than the scale of the hypothesis. His problem is not that a conditional sentence exists in a letter. His problem is waking into pain, counting steps until medication works, choosing footwear around soreness, limiting activity, taking substantial medication and continuing to seek an explanation adequate to the experience. Whenever the record compresses the course into terms such as “improved,” “benefited” or “neuropathic pain,” that scale has to be restored. The documentary object can remain stable while the lived state fluctuates dramatically. The history of the proposition and the history of the pain are not the same line.

This is why the March complaint should be read as an experiential culmination rather than as the manuscript's proof. The patient reaches a conclusion before the documentary genealogy has been fully reconstructed. He experiences what he believes is a failure of reassessment and then asks the institution to recover the history. Only later can the record be examined for the mechanism by which a conditional proposition formed, travelled and retained authority. The two routes may eventually point toward the same concern. Their convergence is meaningful because they arise from different kinds of evidence. It is not self-validating. The complaint remains strongest when it asks a question the documents can independently be made to answer.

That question is increasingly precise. What would a corrigible model have needed to say as the evidence accumulated? It did not necessarily need to declare neuropathy false. It might have said that the old separation no longer organised the whole foot problem, that neurological state clearly contributes, that different foot phenomena appear to have different mechanisms, and that peripheral neuropathy remains only one component requiring independent evidence. Some of that structure is visible implicitly across the later notes. What is harder to locate is the explicit moment at which the founding peripheral architecture is reduced in authority rather than merely supplemented. The complaint makes that absence newly consequential.

By the close of this phase, the patient has begun challenging not just a diagnosis but an inheritance. The neuropathy object remains conditional, clinically usable and capable of coexisting with other explanations. The feet remain painful enough to dominate daily life despite real treatment benefit. Neurological medication repeatedly changes the experience without settling mechanism. The clinician sees a mixed and adaptable picture; the patient sees an old frame that has survived too much contrary pressure. Neither position resolves the history. The next question is institutional: when the history is formally reviewed, what exactly is being reconstructed, and what is merely being inherited again?

## VI. The Object Under Review

The formal complaint changes the institutional setting, but it does not automatically change the evidential object being reviewed. On 25 March 2025, the treating neurologist responds directly to the patient's allegations. His position is not that peripheral neuropathy had ever been conclusively established. He describes it instead as a risk, a worry, or a reasonable possibility, while maintaining that Parkinson's disease had remained central throughout. This distinction matters. The complaint challenges neuropathy as though it had become an organising frame; the response defends it as a legitimate but conditional clinical possibility. The dispute is therefore partly about what level of authority the proposition actually exercised.

The response also insists that the model was not static. Fabry disease had been investigated. Parkinsonian mechanisms for pain were considered. Sinemet was acknowledged to affect the patient's experience of the feet. The neurologist explicitly allows the possibility of being wrong and continues to speak conditionally: “If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here.” That wording is difficult to reconcile with a caricature of diagnostic certainty. It is also precisely why the deeper question cannot be reduced to certainty. A proposition can remain conditional, revisable and clinically influential at the same time. The institutional review therefore has to distinguish between dogma and durable explanatory availability.

The response gives alcohol a similarly qualified role. The neurologist states that the level of alcohol consumption described historically could reasonably contribute to neuropathic symptoms. That is a defensible clinical possibility in general terms, but the evidential genealogy still matters in this particular case. Alcohol entered the record before neuropathy as part of the patient's effort to relax muscles. Later it became a possible cause of peripheral nerve symptoms. By 2025, that causal possibility is defended retrospectively as reasonable. What remains less visible is the chain of case-specific clinical work that would move the proposition from possible contributor to demonstrated explanation. The conditionality survives across all three stages.

For the patient, that response does not resolve the historical question. He is not only disputing whether peripheral neuropathy was medically conceivable. He is asking how the feet came to be organised around that possibility, what evidence supported the separation, and whether later boundary-crossing observations should have reduced its authority. The clinician's response answers some of this indirectly by defending the reasonableness of the evolving clinical model. It does not fully reconstruct the original 2017 information state phrase by phrase. That distinction becomes important as the complaint leaves the treating relationship and enters formal governance.

On 15 April, Quality and Patient Safety assesses the complaint as concerning clinical judgement and forwards it for clinical response. The institutional act is real but narrow. It classifies the complaint procedurally and places the substantive answer back within clinical review. That classification does not independently establish whether the neuropathy proposition was well founded, whether the feet–movement relationship was adequately investigated, or whether the later model was sufficiently corrigible. It establishes what kind of matter the organisation believed it was handling. The difference between procedural allocation and substantive evidential reconstruction should remain visible, because institutional movement can otherwise look like independent re-examination when it may instead be another form of inheritance.

The patient's dissatisfaction then moves the matter into the Incident Management Framework. A Preliminary Assessment Report is prepared and referred onward to the Serious Incident Management Team. The institutional process has escalated. Yet escalation of governance is not the same thing as multiplication of evidence. The crucial question becomes what source material each layer actually reconstructs. Does the new process return to the 2017 contemporaneous record, separate observation from later synthesis, identify the production timing of the neuropathy paragraph, and examine the relation between the dystonic-looking lower limb and the painful feet? Or does it largely inherit later clinical representations of what happened? The surviving scoped packet does not fully answer that.

This distinction is not an accusation against the review. Institutional processes often depend legitimately on summaries, clinician responses and selected source records. A review can reach a defensible governance conclusion without reproducing every step of the original clinical reasoning. But if the patient's challenge concerns the genealogy of an explanatory object, source dependency becomes part of the substantive question. A review of a later representation is not identical to a reconstruction of the earlier information state. The question is therefore not only what conclusion was reached, but what evidence had to travel through how many intermediaries before that conclusion was formed.

The Preliminary Assessment Report was presented to SIMT on 27 August. The final institutional outcome on 3 September states that the clinical decisions were appropriate, that the risks of earlier Sinemet treatment had been considered, that alternative treatment strategies were used, that Sinemet was first prescribed in 2021, and that there was “no deficit in care.” These are consequential institutional findings. They settle the organisation's formal position on the complaint. They do not, simply by existing, answer every narrower evidential question raised by the neuropathy genealogy. Settlement at one jurisdiction can coexist with unresolvedness at another.

The difference is easiest to see in the phrase “no deficit in care.” That conclusion may be supported by information not present in the scoped manuscript corpus, including clinical judgement about treatment risks and acceptable management choices. The available material does not permit this manuscript to substitute itself for an expert standard-of-care review. But “no deficit” cannot manufacture a missing production timestamp for “perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy.” It cannot independently prove whether the alleged Protest occurred. It cannot show, without the underlying review material, whether a specific feet–dystonia comparison was made in 2017. The institutional conclusion therefore has authority within its own decision, but not unlimited authority over every historical unknown.

That is an example of why the manuscript must keep jurisdiction visible without turning theory into the foreground. One question is whether the institution found a care deficit. Another is how the neuropathy object first entered the record. Another is whether later evidence reduced its authority. Another is what the patient was told. Another is what the surviving documents can prove about the timing of synthesis. A conclusion in one domain may bear on another, but it does not automatically settle it. The danger is not that institutional conclusions are false by definition. It is that their scope can be silently enlarged when later readers treat them as answers to questions they were not designed to adjudicate.

The same discipline applies to the data-governance dispute that develops around the historical record. The patient seeks rectification on the basis that bilateral foot pain and the way neuropathy was represented were inaccurate or incomplete. The hospital refuses rectification on the ground that the record accurately preserves the professional opinion documented at the time and remains necessary for continuity of care. That decision says something important about record function. A historical clinical opinion can be retained because it accurately records that the opinion existed, even if the patient disputes its substantive correctness. Documentary accuracy and medical truth are not identical questions.

This creates another layer in the neuropathy object's survival. Once a proposition is part of a historical clinical record, later disagreement does not necessarily remove it. The record may need to preserve what was believed, considered or written at the time. That is legitimate archival logic. But preservation is not the same as continuing clinical authority. A proposition can remain in the chart as historically accurate documentation while losing its power to organise current interpretation. A distinction between retention and governance is therefore necessary. Deleting an old hypothesis may be inappropriate. Allowing an old hypothesis to remain operationally dominant after its evidential basis has weakened would be a different question.

The 2025 governance history does not yet show that distinction being made explicitly. Neuropathy remains present as a reasonable historical possibility. The clinician's response preserves it conditionally. The institutional review finds no care deficit. The rectification decision preserves the historical record. None of those acts necessarily says that neuropathy should continue to organise the whole foot problem prospectively. Yet because each process has a different purpose, the object can survive through all of them without any single process being responsible for reaffirming it as biologically true. This is how documentary persistence can become difficult to interpret: the same phrase survives for different reasons in different systems.

That persistence is especially important because later readers may not see the reasons separately. A clinician may see neuropathy in an old specialist letter. A governance reviewer may see it in a clinician response. A data-protection decision may preserve the same wording because it accurately records historic opinion. A patient may encounter the proposition again while trying to challenge it. Each appearance can look like another institutional confirmation. Yet the evidential roots may remain much fewer than the documentary branches. Repetition across systems can increase apparent solidity even when each system is merely carrying forward what another source had already made available.

This does not mean the later documents are empty repetitions. Each adds something of its own. The clinician response clarifies that neuropathy was never intended as definitive. QPS classifies the issue as clinical judgement. The incident process escalates formal review. SIMT gives an institutional conclusion. The rectification decision distinguishes preservation of historical opinion from rewriting the record. These are new institutional facts. What they do not automatically add is new clinical evidence demonstrating peripheral neuropathy. The genealogy therefore has to keep two columns in view: new information about institutional handling, and new information about the biological or clinical proposition itself. They are not the same stream.

The patient, meanwhile, remains inside both streams. The feet continue to hurt, treatment continues, and the explanatory challenge becomes entangled with requests for access, review and correction. That expansion can make the case look as though it has become mainly administrative. It has not. The administrative activity exists because the clinical object remained unresolved enough for the patient to contest how it had been represented and inherited. Governance cannot be allowed to replace the feet. Every institutional document should be read back against the same embodied question: what is happening in the patient's feet, what evidence supports the available explanations, and what remains clinically unsettled?

The strongest counter-reading also belongs here. A reasonable institution may conclude that care was appropriate because clinicians considered Parkinsonism, structural foot disease, neuropathic possibilities, treatment risk, dystonia, OFF phenomena and later genetic questions while adapting management over time. On that account, the absence of one explicit global reconstruction sentence does not prove a failure to reconsider. Medicine often works through evolving mixed models rather than formal declarations that old hypotheses have been demoted. That explanation cannot be dismissed. The question is whether the surviving record shows enough active reassessment that the neuropathy object remained only appropriately provisional rather than improperly governing.

What keeps the question open is the object's unusual combination of weakness and durability. It remains grammatically conditional. It never becomes a clearly demonstrated peripheral-neuropathy diagnosis in the disclosed corpus. Yet it persists through clinical notes, treatment logic, overlap investigation, complaint response, governance and data-preservation disputes. That persistence may simply reflect the continued plausibility of a mixed mechanism. It may also reflect documentary inertia. The available evidence does not yet permit the manuscript to choose between those explanations completely. It does permit a sharper question: what would we expect to see if the object had genuinely lost organising authority, even while remaining historically preserved?

A corrigible record might not erase the old proposition. It might instead mark a transition: neuropathy remains possible, but accumulated evidence now shows that neurological state materially contributes to the feet; the old separation should no longer organise the problem as a whole. Some later notes approach that position indirectly. The clinician's “misalignment” comment, OFF-dystonia reasoning and acknowledgment of Sinemet-responsive pain all move in that direction. What is difficult to locate is a clear documentary de-governance of the founding peripheral architecture. The distinction matters because persistence in the record can otherwise be mistaken for persistence in evidential entitlement.

By the end of the institutional review, the neuropathy object has survived another kind of pressure. It has been challenged by the patient, defended as reasonable but conditional by the clinician, reviewed procedurally, preserved historically and left compatible with an institutional finding of no care deficit. None of that proves the object was correct. None proves it was improperly retained. What it shows is how an explanatory proposition can remain available across systems whose purposes differ. The next movement turns back toward biology, because later external and genetic evidence will clarify parts of the neurological picture in ways the earlier record could not know, while raising a new question about what old explanations should now be allowed to keep governing.

## VII. Later Biological Clarification

The institutional conclusion did not end the clinical history. In July 2025, a further referral was sought because the patient was questioning the existing account and wanted specialist reconsideration of the movement disorder, including dystonia. That referral should not be treated as proof that the earlier diagnosis was wrong. Its importance is more immediate. After years in which the feet had accumulated structural, painful, neuropathic and Parkinsonian descriptions, the patient was seeking another neurological reading of the same body. The request itself shows that the explanatory problem had not closed for him simply because an institutional process had reached a conclusion.

The external pathway also changes the provenance of what follows. Until this point, much of the later reconsideration still comes from within the same longitudinal clinical system that inherited the 2017 history. A second opinion has the potential to become another evidential root rather than another branch, but only to the extent that its observations and conclusions can be independently recovered. Here the surviving source packet imposes a limit. The patient later reports receiving an authoritative dystonia diagnosis in December 2025, but the primary document establishing that consultation is not presently available in the scoped corpus. The later report proves the allegation exists. It does not permit reconstruction of the examination that earned it.

That gap matters because dystonia has already become powerful in hindsight. The 2017 specialist had described the gait as somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture. OFF dystonia entered the treating record years later. Toe curling persisted when pregabalin reduced pain. Movement state repeatedly altered the feet. An external dystonia diagnosis would therefore appear to connect several previously separated observations. But the manuscript cannot use a missing primary source merely because its reported conclusion would fit the developing pattern. The right response to convergence is not automatic belief or automatic scepticism. It is provenance. What was diagnosed, by whom, on what examination, and in which source remains necessary.

The position changes materially with the later genetic evidence. CeGaT testing identifies two pathogenic variants in **PRKN**, including an exon 2 deletion and the c.1289G>A, p.Gly430Asp variant. The laboratory interpretation is conditional on the variants being situated in the disease-causing configuration required for recessive PRKN disease. Subsequent specialist documentation treats the two variants as compound heterozygous and causative. By February 2026, the clinical formulation is direct: the Parkinsonism and dystonia are attributed to compound heterozygous mutations in Parkin. This is not another inherited description of the 2017 foot problem. It is a new evidential root concerning the underlying movement disorder.

The genetic result has unusual force because PRKN was not invented retrospectively as a convenient explanation. PARK2 had already appeared in the 2017 specialist differential. In September of that year, Professor Daniel Healy advised that the patient “probably has Park II” and recommended PARK2 testing before moving to a broader panel if necessary. The early clinicians therefore had already recognised a phenotype capable of making Parkin disease a live genetic question. The 2026 result validates the importance of that question. It does not establish that genetic confirmation should necessarily have occurred at any particular earlier date. That is a different question requiring contemporaneous standards, access and clinical judgement.

This distinction prevents the genetics from becoming an instrument of hindsight. It would be easy to write that the answer was present all along. It was not. In 2017 there was a very young patient with severe Parkinsonism, unusual lower-limb phenomenology and a genetic differential that included PARK2. In 2026 there is molecular evidence supporting PRKN disease. Those are different information states. The later result cannot be carried backwards as though the clinicians at the bedside already possessed it. What it can do is change the present interpretation of facts they really did possess, particularly the lower-limb abnormalities that were observed before neuropathy had become an enduring explanatory object.

The dystonic-looking posture is the clearest example. In 2017 it was an examination observation: an unusual gait, resembling foot drop in some respects, but somewhat suggestive of dystonia and explicitly not true foot drop. The observation did not then establish the cause of the foot pain. Later PRKN evidence still does not perform that impossible conversion. What changes is the surrounding model. Dystonia is no longer merely one speculative reading of an unusual posture. It has become part of a genetically grounded neurological disorder attributed to Parkin mutations. The old observation now has a more secure biological context, even though its exact relationship to the historical pain remains unresolved.

That difference is subtle but decisive. Later evidence can strengthen the relevance of an earlier observation without becoming earlier evidence. The 2017 examiner genuinely saw something dystonic-looking. The 2026 genetics can make that observation more intelligible now. They cannot prove that every corn, callus, burning sensation or episode of pain was dystonic then. Nor can they show what would have happened if a different investigative path had been followed. The new evidence alters the architecture of the present explanation. It does not rewrite the content of the past. A historical source remains entitled only to what it could carry at the time.

The same discipline must apply to the neuropathy object. PRKN confirmation does not disprove peripheral neuropathy. The patient could still have structural foot disease, neuropathic pain, a peripheral neuropathy, dystonia and Parkinsonian sensory modulation in various combinations. Nothing about recessive Parkin disease requires every symptom to share one mechanism. The question is not whether neuropathy becomes impossible. It is whether the amount of authority it is entitled to exercise over the **whole foot problem** should change once Parkinsonism and dystonia themselves have received a substantially stronger biological foundation than the peripheral-neuropathy proposition ever did.

That comparison exposes an important asymmetry. The neuropathy object began as “perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy,” supported by a mixture of symptom description, historical alcohol exposure, structural feet and patient-attributed separation. It remained conditional. No confirming neurophysiology is visible in the scoped record. The later PRKN object, by contrast, rests on identified pathogenic variants and a specialist interpretation integrating Parkinsonism and dystonia. These propositions need not compete for exclusive truth. They simply do not occupy the same evidential level. Continued coexistence should therefore not be mistaken for continued equivalence.

A corrigible model should be able to respond to that change in evidential weight. It does not need to delete neuropathy from the history. Historical hypotheses should not vanish merely because a stronger explanation later appears. Nor should a possible peripheral contribution be declared false without evidence. But the organising hierarchy can change. A proposition once used to place the feet partly outside the Parkinsonian presentation may become a narrower residual possibility inside a model now capable of explaining much more of the lower-limb phenomenology neurologically. De-governance is not erasure. It is a reduction in what an inherited proposition is allowed to organise.

The record makes this distinction especially necessary because the original neuropathy object was never completely rigid. Its grammar always contained an escape route: “perhaps,” “Not clear,” later “if there is a degree.” That caution is clinically valuable. It protects against a false assertion of certainty. Yet the same open-endedness can make it difficult to identify the moment at which the proposition should lose influence. A hypothesis that says only “perhaps” can survive almost any contrary development because it never claimed enough to be straightforwardly falsified. Corrigibility therefore requires something more than uncertainty. It requires a sense of what evidence would make the hypothesis matter less.

The later history supplies several candidates. Neurological medication altered a focal foot territory during procyclidine withdrawal and restoration. A later apparent foot drop was judged Parkinsonian after direct power testing. OFF dystonia became an explicit clinical possibility. Leg pain and shuffling were judged OFF-related. Morning foot suffering repeatedly changed as dopaminergic medication took effect. Toe curling persisted after substantial pregabalin pain relief. Finally, PRKN disease provided a molecular context in which dystonia belongs naturally to the neurological syndrome. None is individually sufficient to abolish neuropathy. Together they make continued unrestricted separation harder to sustain.

The word **unrestricted** matters. The clinical system did not continue to treat the feet as wholly outside Parkinsonism. The later record plainly shows the opposite. Parkinsonian contributions were recognised; OFF phenomena were treated; dystonia was considered; levodopa was used partly with the morning feet in mind. To claim that the original separation remained completely intact would flatten genuine clinical change. What appears more persistent is not an absolute boundary but the continued availability of peripheral neuropathy as an explanatory component. The question is therefore becoming finer: did that component shrink appropriately as neurological evidence strengthened, or did its original documentary weight remain easier to inherit than its evidential basis warranted?

The answer is difficult partly because clinical notes are not written as formal model revisions. They record encounters, symptoms, decisions and plans. A clinician may substantially change their internal weighting of causes without ever writing, “the former neuropathy hypothesis now carries less authority.” Silence cannot prove that no such change occurred. At the same time, documentary history matters precisely because later care depends on what is communicated. If a proposition has genuinely receded, the record may eventually be expected to show that receding through changed terminology, altered differential emphasis, explicit qualification, or disappearance from decisions that once depended on it. Otherwise the old object can remain available by default.

The genetics therefore create a useful test of the record's capacity to distinguish historical preservation from current authority. A 2017 statement about possible neuropathy should remain visible as part of the clinical history. It may still matter to current care if peripheral disease remains genuinely possible. But a later reader should also be able to see that the explanatory landscape has changed substantially. Parkin disease is no longer a speculation. Dystonia is no longer merely an isolated descriptive possibility. The lower-limb neurological phenotype has acquired molecular support. If those changes do not alter how the older separation is read, the record risks preserving chronology without preserving evidential hierarchy.

The patient's position also changes in light of this later evidence, but not into proof that his earlier complaint was correct in every respect. His March 2025 challenge preceded the genetic result. He did not know the molecular answer when he alleged that the feet had been insufficiently reconsidered. That temporal order matters. The genetics cannot be used to manufacture certainty inside his earlier allegation. What they can do is create independent later convergence with one part of his concern: the neurological system eventually acquired stronger evidence for lower-limb dystonia than was available when the original neuropathy explanation was constructed. His experiential route and the later biological route meet, but they remain distinct.

That distinction protects the manuscript from converting vindication into omniscience. A later molecular diagnosis can confirm that a person was right to question an explanatory boundary without proving that every detail of their retrospective causal account was correct. The patient may have correctly sensed that the feet were neurologically entangled while still being wrong about the mechanism of some pain. He may have had real structural pathology and possible peripheral symptoms at the same time. The importance of the later evidence lies not in producing a single total cause. It lies in making a simple allocation of “neurological disease here, separate feet there” progressively less defensible as an organising summary.

There is another reason the PRKN result matters. It returns the case to a question that existed near the beginning but was never the same as neuropathy. In 2017, the unusual age and phenotype prompted PARK2 reasoning. Neuropathy later entered the genetic differential through PARK9 as “the neuropathy question.” Those are two different routes into genetics. One route arose from the core Parkinsonian phenotype. The other used the possible peripheral feature as a clue toward another rare disorder. The eventual result favoured the first route. That does not make the second unreasonable. It does show that the higher-order use of neuropathy did not ultimately receive the same molecular confirmation.

The difference allows the genealogy to be read more cleanly. “The neuropathy question” gained conceptual prominence in 2017 because it could help organise the search for a rare genetic explanation. Years later, the confirmed genetic explanation instead integrates Parkinsonism with dystonia through PRKN. Neuropathy may remain beside that result, but it no longer performs the same higher-order work. The case no longer needs peripheral neuropathy to make the unusual neurological phenotype genetically intelligible. One of the functions the neuropathy object acquired early in its life has therefore genuinely diminished, even if the possibility of a peripheral component has not disappeared.

This is the first place where something like release becomes visible without needing to force theoretical language onto the case. An explanation can remain historically present while ceasing to carry the same determining force. It need not be disproved. It need not be deleted. It simply no longer gets to organise as much as it once did. The possibility of neuropathy may still bear on pain management, sensory symptoms or future investigation. What it should no longer do without new supporting evidence is stand in for the unresolved relationship between the feet and the neurological disease as a whole. The later record has become too rich for that compression.

That conclusion does not depend on deciding whether the original 2017 hypothesis met the standard of care. The historical question of what clinicians reasonably should have investigated then requires specialist evidence about contemporary practice. The present documentary question is different. Looking longitudinally, the evidential basis supporting neurological involvement of the feet has grown considerably. The evidential basis visibly demonstrating peripheral neuropathy has not grown at the same rate. Whatever judgement one makes about the reasonableness of the original hypothesis, those two trajectories are no longer symmetrical. A record capable of preserving change should allow that difference to become visible rather than letting the older possibility retain authority merely through age.

The difference between age and evidence is central to the entire history. Old propositions often feel foundational because they occur near the beginning. Repeated propositions feel corroborated because they occur in many places. Specialist propositions feel stronger because they are embedded in sophisticated reasoning. Treated propositions feel validated because clinicians acted on them. Preserved propositions feel institutionally endorsed because they survive review and data governance. None of those transformations is necessarily illegitimate. But none is identical to fresh clinical confirmation. The neuropathy object has accumulated several forms of durability while remaining remarkably resistant to becoming an independently demonstrated fact.

That durability helps explain why the patient's challenge became larger than a disagreement over diagnosis. From his side, the feet remained the lived constant. They hurt before neuropathy existed in the record. They continued to hurt while neuropathy remained possible. They changed with neurological medication. They restricted walking, footwear, activity and daily life even when some treatments helped. Later biology strengthened the neurological account of the lower limbs. What he repeatedly encountered, however, was not a single decisive point at which the old peripheral possibility was either confirmed or reduced to a clearly bounded residual role. The unresolved feet outlived several explanatory reorganisations around them.

The record's compression can make that difficult to see. A sequence of notes can show pregabalin benefit, OFF dystonia, Parkinsonian foot movement, Fabry testing, conditional neuropathy, Sinemet response and later PRKN disease. Read as a list, this looks like comprehensive adaptation—and in many respects it is. Read as an explanatory genealogy, a different question appears. Which observations created new roots? Which merely added branches? Which altered treatment? Which altered the organising model? Which reduced the authority of older propositions? The same record can demonstrate extensive clinical activity while leaving those questions only partially answered. Activity and reconstruction are not synonyms.

The later biological clarification therefore does not finish the neuropathy story. It changes the terms under which that story can continue. Peripheral neuropathy may still exist. Neuropathic treatment may still be useful. Structural disease certainly remains historically real. But the neurological component of the feet is no longer an awkward possibility at the edge of the case. Parkinsonian foot movement has been observed, OFF-related pain has been judged clinically relevant, dystonia has entered the treating model, and PRKN disease has supplied a molecular explanation for Parkinsonism with dystonia. The burden of explanation has shifted. Continued peripheral attribution now has to coexist with stronger evidence on the other side of the old boundary.

What should give way under those conditions is not necessarily a diagnosis but an allocation. The earliest specialist synthesis made it possible to imagine the painful feet as completely separate, perhaps peripheral, perhaps alcohol-related, and strongly distinguished in the patient's own mind from the leg tension. Later history does not permit that formulation to remain untouched. Even if peripheral neuropathy ultimately proves to coexist, the feet have demonstrated too many neurological relations for complete separation to remain an adequate organising description. The old proposition can survive only in a narrower jurisdiction. It may explain something. It can no longer, without further evidence, be allowed to explain the separation itself.

That is the point at which the history begins to reveal the structure named by the title. The illusion need not be that neuropathy was false. It need not be that anyone fabricated it, or that clinicians ceased thinking, or that the patient had one simple neurological pain syndrome all along. The more exact possibility is that different forms of durability gradually became hard to distinguish from evidential establishment. A clinically possible proposition became documentarily stable, genetically useful, therapeutically compatible, institutionally preserved and repeatedly available. Those are real properties. None, alone or together, is the same thing as demonstrating peripheral neuropathy in the patient.

Once those properties accumulate, the origin becomes easy to lose. The later reader encounters a mature object and may no longer feel how little had originally been settled. “Neuropathy” has by then acquired years of history around it. Alcohol has been discussed beside it. Pregabalin has been prescribed beside it. Fabry disease has been investigated beside it. Complaints and responses have repeated it. Governance processes have encountered it. The word has survived long enough to look as though it has always belonged to the case. But its first appearance still returns us to the same small set of questions: where was the sensory evidence, what was actually examined, what did “burning” mean, and what work earned separation?

The answer remains incomplete. That incompleteness is not proof of wrongdoing. It is the evidential fact around which the manuscript has been circling. The visible record shows a highly sophisticated investigation of very-young-onset Parkinsonism. It shows genuine structural foot disease. It shows a plausible peripheral hypothesis. It shows an observed dystonic-looking lower limb. It shows a sophisticated explanation in which the feet can become separate, neuropathic and possibly alcohol-related. What it shows less clearly is an equally sophisticated investigation of the relation among those facts before the separation becomes portable. The explanatory object is more complete than the surviving account of the clinical work that earned it.

Later history does not close that gap simply by adding more documents. In some respects it makes the gap easier to see. The more evidence accumulates that the feet participate in the neurological disorder, the more consequential the original relation becomes. Yet later evidence cannot be sent backwards to convict an earlier uncertainty. Its proper role is different. It tests the inherited model's corrigibility. It asks whether an explanation constructed under uncertainty remained capable of surrendering authority when new relations became visible. That is not a question about whether clinicians were allowed to be uncertain. It is a question about what uncertainty is supposed to do when the world keeps answering back.

## VIII. The Illusion

The word **illusion** becomes useful only at the point where several different kinds of truth have accumulated around the same proposition. Peripheral neuropathy was clinically conceivable. That is one truth. It was written into a specialist record. That is another. It remained available over time. That is another. Treatments associated with neuropathic pain later helped some of the suffering. That is another. The patient continued to have feet capable of producing sensory language, structural pain and neurological phenomena. That is another. The difficulty begins when those truths become difficult to distinguish from a different proposition: that peripheral neuropathy had itself been firmly demonstrated as the organising explanation of the feet.

Nothing in the history requires deception for that confusion to occur. A proposition can gain weight simply by surviving. It is written once in an authoritative context, then encountered later as part of the known case. It is carried because it remains possible. It is repeated because it remains relevant. It is acted on because treatment can be reasonable under uncertainty. It is preserved because historical records should not be rewritten merely because their conclusions are later disputed. Each step is individually intelligible. Taken together, however, they can make the proposition appear older, firmer and more independently supported than the chain of evidence actually shows.

The difference between a root and a branch is therefore not merely technical. The first explicit neuropathy proposition arose inside a specialist synthesis that also contained real structural disease, significant alcohol history, a newly visible description of “burning,” and a patient-attributed distinction between foot pain and leg tension. Later references do not automatically recreate that evidential foundation. Some add new observations. Some add treatment response. Some add competing neurological mechanisms. Some simply preserve the possibility. If those later appearances are counted as though each were another independent confirmation, the genealogy disappears. Continuity of documentation begins to resemble continuity of proof.

The phrase “neuropathic pain” is particularly vulnerable to that transformation because it can operate at several levels. It may describe a quality of pain. It may name the category of medication being used. It may indicate a working mechanism. It may function as shorthand in a busy clinical note. It may also be inherited from earlier language without fresh testing. Those uses are not identical, even when they produce similar words on the page. Occurrences cannot simply be counted. What matters is what each one adds. A repeated adjective may preserve an explanatory field without strengthening the underlying diagnosis at all.

Treatment response creates a similar hazard. Pregabalin helping pain is a clinically important fact. It can justify continuing a treatment that gives meaningful relief. It may support the practical judgement that some component of the pain behaves in a way compatible with neuropathic modulation. It does not operate as a diagnostic confirmation of peripheral neuropathy. The same is true in the opposite direction. Sinemet helping the feet does not prove that every painful symptom was Parkinsonian or dystonic. Treatments can alter symptoms through mechanisms broader than the disease category under which they were prescribed. Response adds evidence. It does not remove the need to specify what kind of evidence it adds.

This is where preserving scale becomes part of preserving causation. A compressed note that says the patient “benefited from pregabalin” may be perfectly accurate. If the surrounding scale is lost, the sentence can imply much more: adequate control, satisfactory function, even successful explanation. Yet the later history still contains a life described as much smaller, severe morning suffering, difficulty with footwear, pain dominating the first steps of the day and continued need for substantial treatment. Therapeutic benefit and unresolved suffering coexist. The illusion of resolution can arise not because the benefit is false, but because the baseline and residual burden fall out of the inherited sentence.

The same compression can affect the original symptom history. A later reader encountering “burning” inside a paragraph about possible neuropathy may experience the word as though it had always belonged to the presenting complaint. The surviving pre-specialist material does not establish that. It records pain, corns, callouses, abnormal gait, toe clawing, stiffness, tension and functional decline. “Burning” becomes documentarily visible later in the specialist synthesis. That does not prove the patient never used the word earlier. It does mean the record cannot safely project it backwards. A symptom descriptor can appear clinically natural once the diagnosis beside it has become familiar, even when its first documentary provenance remains uncertain.

“Markedly divorced” creates a different illusion: one of authorship. The completed specialist product says the patient had, in his own mind, markedly divorced the tension from the corns, callouses and foot pain. There is support for a genuine patient-perceived difference. His historical account distinguished symptoms that changed differently over time. But the exact formulation is clinician-authored text. Without an independent patient source using equivalent language, the record cannot show that the patient himself framed the distinction with the same strength or explanatory consequences. A clinician can accurately summarise a patient's meaning and still create a sentence whose later readers experience as the patient's own proposition.

That difference matters because patient attribution carries persuasive force. If the patient himself regarded the feet as completely separate, later clinical separation may look less like an imposed model and more like recognition of his own account. That may in fact be what happened. The problem is that the surviving evidence permits a weaker statement more securely than the stronger one. The patient appears to have distinguished foot pain from leg tension experientially. The document characterises that distinction as marked divorce. The biological independence of the phenomena remains a further inference. Each step may be reasonable. They are still separate steps.

Alcohol underwent a comparable change in function. Early in the record, alcohol appears as part of the patient's attempt to relax his muscles and sleep. In the specialist synthesis, significant historical intake becomes a possible contributor to peripheral neuropathy. By 2025, the clinician defends that possibility as medically reasonable. None of those uses is inherently contradictory. What changes is the question alcohol is being asked to answer. A historical behaviour becomes an etiological clue. Later, that etiological clue becomes part of the retrospective defence of the model. The fact remains the same while its explanatory role grows. Unless the transitions are kept visible, the cause can appear to have been present in the history from the beginning.

The most consequential transformation occurs when the neuropathy question rises into genetic reasoning. “Perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy” is a local hypothesis about the feet and legs. “PARK9 — the neuropathy question” gives the proposition a second-order function. It becomes a clue about what type of young-onset Parkinsonian disorder might be present. That does not mean the clinician treated neuropathy as established. Differential diagnosis routinely uses provisional features. Yet the higher-order reasoning can make the underlying feature feel more substantial. A question capable of reorganising the genetic differential looks important, and importance is easy to confuse with evidential solidity.

The eventual PRKN result shows why those two qualities must remain separate. The genetic route that received molecular support was the PARK2/PRKN route already prompted by the core Parkinsonian phenotype, not the PARK9 route prompted by the neuropathy question. That does not retrospectively invalidate the PARK9 consideration. It does, however, reveal that conceptual prominence and later confirmation can diverge. A provisional feature may be important enough to explore and still not become the feature that ultimately explains the disease. The record becomes easier to understand once explanatory usefulness is allowed to remain distinct from evidential verification.

The illusion also concerns the sophistication of the document itself. The 2017 specialist product is clinically rich. It recognises severe young-onset Parkinsonism, considers rare genetic disease, records structural foot abnormalities, takes an extensive history, interprets unusual gait and incorporates later imaging. It is easy for the sophistication of that whole product to confer authority on every relation expressed within it. But a document can be sophisticated in one domain and comparatively thin in another. The investigation of Parkinsonism was highly developed. The visible investigation of whether the painful feet were structurally independent, neurologically driven, peripherally neuropathic, dystonic, loading-related, or mixed was less clearly developed.

This is not a contradiction. Specialists often have to form working explanations without exhaustive testing of every symptom relationship. A plausible hypothesis can be the right clinical tool even when evidence is incomplete. The important point is jurisdiction. The diagnosis of Parkinsonism may be strongly grounded while the mechanism of the feet remains less settled. If the first completion leaks into the second, the reader can experience the entire discharge product as though all of its internal relationships were equally earned. The sophistication of the whole becomes a proxy for the certainty of each part. That is one of the central documentary illusions in the case.

The patient then lives inside the consequences of that asymmetry. His Parkinsonian disorder becomes increasingly determinate. His feet remain less so. They can be structurally abnormal, painful, sensitive, medication-responsive, dystonic-looking, Parkinsonian in movement, neuropathic in treatment language and still not yield a single complete account. The record continues to adapt around them. What the patient experiences, however, is not the elegance of a mixed model. He experiences the fact that the feet remain capable of dominating mornings, movement, footwear, work, recreation and psychological endurance. The explanatory object travels more steadily than the lived problem resolves.

That difference helps explain why later institutional review and clinical adaptation can both be genuine without satisfying the patient's demand. From the clinical side, many things were reconsidered. Parkinsonian pain was recognised. OFF dystonia was considered. Medication was adjusted. Fabry disease was investigated. Pregabalin was escalated. Sinemet was used with the feet in mind. From the patient's side, none of that necessarily answers how the original separation was earned or when its authority was reduced. One history records interventions. The other asks about the organising model underneath them. Both histories can be true at once.

The absence of an explicit exit condition becomes more important as those histories diverge. What evidence, if observed, would have made peripheral neuropathy stop organising the feet? A normal test might have narrowed it, had the relevant test been performed and been sufficiently sensitive to the suspected condition. A clear movement-state dependence might have shifted the balance. A repeatedly demonstrated dystonic mechanism might have done so. A molecular diagnosis linking Parkinsonism and dystonia might have done so. Yet because the original proposition was always cautious and mixed mechanisms remained possible, almost any new evidence could be accommodated beside it rather than against it.

That flexibility can be clinically sophisticated. It can also make a model difficult to de-govern. If peripheral neuropathy is only one possible component, then neurological evidence does not falsify it. If pregabalin helps, that can sit beside dopamine responsiveness. If toe curling persists, that can be assigned to dystonia while pain remains peripheral. If structural lesions remain, they can explain loading while neuropathy explains burning. Mixed models are often the right models. The difficulty arises only if mixture becomes a structure in which no observation can ever reduce an inherited component's authority. A model can be open-minded and still require rules for letting old possibilities recede.

The case therefore turns corrigibility into a practical rather than philosophical question. A clinical model is not corrigible merely because it contains words like “perhaps.” It is corrigible when later evidence can change not only what is added, but what is allowed to govern. Correction does not always mean replacing A with B. It can mean narrowing A, demoting it, limiting its jurisdiction, or recognising that it explains one component but not the whole. The record shows many additions. What it shows less clearly is the moment when the original peripheral separation is explicitly narrowed in response to the growing neurological account of the feet.

That narrowing may have occurred in practice without being written in a single sentence. The later clinician's “misalignment” formulation comes close. The patient thinks the feet are separate; the clinician believes much can be explained by Parkinson's disease. OFF dystonia enters. Sinemet-related pain relief is acknowledged. Those are not trivial changes. They show that the old boundary has become porous. But because neuropathy remains present conditionally, the longitudinal reader still has to infer how much authority it has lost. The record adapts locally more clearly than it declares its new global hierarchy.

The distinction between preservation and authority becomes crucial here. A historical record should preserve that peripheral neuropathy was considered. Erasing the proposition because later evidence favours another explanation would falsify the history. The same proposition may even remain medically possible. But preservation answers one question: what was thought? Current authority answers another: what should organise interpretation now? Confusing the two can make an old hypothesis seem to retain clinical force simply because it properly remains visible in the chart. A document can be historically accurate and prospectively misleading if its old hierarchy is inherited without its original uncertainty and later revision.

This is why the data-governance dispute cannot resolve the medical genealogy by itself. A decision to retain the original professional opinion may be appropriate because the record accurately documents that opinion. That says nothing definitive about whether the opinion should continue to organise the present feet. Conversely, a patient request to correct the historical record may express a legitimate concern about current meaning while still asking for something archival practice cannot simply provide. The case contains two different problems: how to preserve what clinicians actually wrote, and how to prevent preserved historical propositions from acquiring unearned present authority.

The institutional conclusion has a similar limit. “No deficit in care” may settle the organisation's review of whether clinical management fell below an acceptable standard. The available material does not provide a basis for overturning that conclusion as a standard-of-care judgement. Yet that settlement does not answer the narrower genealogy. It does not tell us when “burning” was first elicited, whether the patient was told about neuropathy during admission, what examination specifically supported the peripheral hypothesis, when the relevant phrases were drafted, or whether a feet–dystonia comparison was consciously performed. Those questions remain what they were before the governance process: historical and evidential.

The alleged Protest sits inside that unresolved space. If the patient did tell the team before discharge that the bilateral foot problem remained inadequately addressed, the event would matter because it would show the unresolved relationship being raised while the specialist product was still forming. If the neuropathy/separation paragraph was written after that protest, the chronology would become especially important. If it was written before, a different interpretation would follow. But the disclosed record does not currently establish the exchange contemporaneously, and the production dates of the key phrases remain unknown. The Protest cannot be used to solve a chronology the sources do not solve.

Even bracketed out entirely, however, the central asymmetry remains. The GP referred an unresolved feet–gait–tone–movement presentation. Neurology observed a dystonic-looking lower limb. The completed product developed a sophisticated route through separation, possible neuropathy, alcohol and patient-attributed distinction. The visible record does not show an equally sophisticated investigation of the relation between the painful structural feet and the abnormal neurological movement before that explanatory route was completed. The Protest could strengthen the significance of that gap. The gap does not depend on the Protest for its existence.

This is why the title can finally be stated without implying fraud. **The Neurology Neuropathy Illusion** names the possibility that an explanatory object acquired the appearance of greater evidential establishment than its original support warranted because several other properties accumulated around it: specialist authorship, conceptual usefulness, documentary durability, therapeutic compatibility, repetition, institutional preservation and longevity. Those properties are not fake. The illusion lies in how easily they can be mistaken for one another when the genealogy is no longer visible.

It is also an illusion of completed relationship. Once Parkinsonism is diagnosed, the neurological problem feels solved. Once Podiatry treats structural lesions, the feet feel allocated. Once neuropathy becomes plausible, sensory pain has a route. Once alcohol supplies a possible cause, the peripheral account gains coherence. Once the patient is described as having “markedly divorced” the phenomena, the separation acquires personal authorship. Each move reduces uncertainty locally. Together they can create the appearance that the relation among the feet, gait, tone and movement has been adequately resolved. Yet the later history repeatedly reopens precisely that relation.

The subsequent evidence does not reopen it because the original clinicians necessarily acted unreasonably. It reopens it because living systems continue to produce information after documents are finished. The foot territory changes with neurological treatment. An apparent foot drop is later judged Parkinsonian. OFF dystonia becomes clinically relevant. Dopaminergic state alters pain and walking. Pregabalin changes pain without changing toe curling. PRKN disease later integrates Parkinsonism and dystonia. Each new observation has limited scope, but together they show that the old boundary was never the final word. The patient kept generating evidence after the explanation had become history.

That is where Completed Intelligibility enters the case without needing to dominate it. A completion is not wrong because it later becomes insufficient. It is entitled to settle the demand it actually answered. The 2017 specialist work could legitimately settle that the patient had severe young-onset Parkinsonism while leaving the feet mechanism open. It could legitimately make peripheral neuropathy a possibility without demonstrating it. It could legitimately separate some experiences phenomenologically without proving biological independence. Trouble begins only when one completion silently inherits the jurisdiction of another question.

The same principle applies later. Pregabalin can complete a treatment question—does this reduce some of the pain?—without completing an etiological one. Sinemet can complete an observation—does dopaminergic state change the foot experience?—without proving exclusive causation. PRKN can complete a molecular question about Parkinsonism and dystonia without retroactively proving the mechanism of every painful foot episode. SIMT can complete an institutional review without resolving every provenance gap. Each completion is real. Each has a boundary. The history becomes distorted when those boundaries disappear.

The neuropathy object is therefore most revealing not as a diagnosis but as a carrier of misplaced jurisdiction. It may have been entitled to govern the question “is a peripheral component clinically plausible?” It was never shown in the disclosed record to have earned unrestricted authority over “why are the feet painful?” or “how are the feet related to the neurological disorder?” Those larger questions continued producing contradictory, supplementary and boundary-crossing evidence for years. A proposition can remain valid inside its proper jurisdiction and still become misleading when allowed to organise beyond it.

The later history also makes visible a form of release. An old proposition does not need to be deleted to stop governing. It can remain true as history, remain possible as medicine and remain relevant to one symptom dimension while losing authority over the whole. That is a more precise endpoint than vindication or refutation. The available evidence does not establish that peripheral neuropathy never existed. It does establish that the neurological account of the lower limbs became progressively stronger and more specific. The rational longitudinal response is not necessarily to erase neuropathy, but to make its jurisdiction smaller unless new evidence enlarges it again.

Such release is difficult in records because records privilege persistence. What is written stays written. What is copied stays visible. What is coded or summarised can outlive the uncertainty that originally surrounded it. Human clinicians may revise their thinking while the documentary object remains textually stable. The result is a mismatch between epistemic change and archival continuity. The task is not to accuse the archive of lying, but to reconstruct the changing authority that the archive alone does not automatically display.

The patient's feet reveal that mismatch with unusual force because they never became merely historical. They continued to hurt while interpretations shifted. They continued to limit life while treatments helped partially. They remained capable of producing new neurological relations while the old peripheral possibility survived. The body therefore functioned as a source of correction long after the first specialist product had been completed. The question is whether the documentary system could absorb those corrections not just as additional facts, but as reasons to change what earlier facts were allowed to mean.

That question has no single dramatic answer in the record. There is no visible sentence in which the old model is formally withdrawn. Nor is there a visible sentence in which clinicians insist that neuropathy must remain the dominant explanation despite contrary evidence. What exists is messier: conditional persistence, genuine adaptation, mixed mechanisms, changing treatment, new observations, patient disagreement, institutional review and later biological clarification. The absence of a clean reversal is part of the point. Explanatory objects often lose authority gradually, locally and incompletely.

For that reason, the strongest conclusion remains narrower than the strongest accusation. The disclosed record does not establish that neuropathy was invented to dispose of an inconvenient symptom. It does not establish that clinicians acted in bad faith, ignored all contrary evidence or refused to reconsider. It does not establish that every historical foot symptom was neurological. It does not establish that alcohol was irrelevant or that peripheral disease was absent. What it does support is a more exact asymmetry: the explanation that allowed the feet to be carried as possibly separate and neuropathic became more developed than the visible investigation of the specific relation between the painful feet and the abnormal neurological movement that accompanied them.

The later history increases the significance of that asymmetry without converting it into retrospective proof of error. Neurological involvement of the feet becomes progressively harder to treat as marginal. Yet the peripheral proposition remains conditional rather than decisively tested. In that setting, repetition can obscure origin. Treatment can obscure mechanism. Institutional preservation can obscure current authority. Improvement can obscure residual suffering. Sophisticated general investigation can obscure a narrower unanswered relation. The illusion is created by these substitutions, not by a single false statement.

The final question therefore returns to the beginning. Before neuropathy, there were painful abnormal feet, unusual gait, increased tone, toe clawing, initiation difficulty and declining function. Neurology received a relationship that had not yet been explained. Peripheral neuropathy entered as one way of making part of that relationship intelligible. It then travelled farther than the original encounter, carrying its uncertainty with it but also acquiring new forms of usefulness and durability. The history since then has supplied reasons to narrow its reach, but not necessarily to erase it.

What matters most is not whether the word remains in the record. It is whether the unresolved question that preceded the word can still be recovered. If the reader can see that question again, the chronology changes shape. The feet cease to look like a settled peripheral problem attached to a settled neurological diagnosis. They become what they were before the documentary architecture hardened: a painful, structurally abnormal, movement-sensitive part of a much larger neurological presentation whose relations had to be earned, not assumed.

That recovery is the corrective act available to a later record. It does not require rewriting history. It requires restoring scale, provenance, uncertainty and jurisdiction. It requires remembering which observations were direct, which were attributed, which were inferred, which were inherited and which were produced later. It requires letting new evidence change the authority of old explanations without pretending the old explanations were never reasonable. Above all, it requires refusing to confuse the durability of an explanatory object with the adequacy of the investigation that first allowed it to govern.

The patient did not spend eight years inside the phrase “possible peripheral neuropathy.” He spent them inside his feet. The proposition travelled through letters, consultations, treatments, investigations, complaint responses and institutional processes. The pain travelled differently: sometimes less, sometimes more, sometimes altered by pregabalin, sometimes altered by dopaminergic state, sometimes accompanied by toe curling, sometimes severe enough to contract ordinary life around it. The two histories repeatedly touched. They were never identical. Any account that follows only the proposition risks losing the suffering. Any account that follows only the suffering risks losing the evidential discipline needed to understand how the proposition acquired authority.

The case becomes intelligible only when both histories remain in view. One asks what happened to the explanatory object. The other asks what happened to the patient while that object remained available. Between them lies the longitudinal test: did the model become more corrigible as the body produced new information, or did new information accumulate around an inherited structure whose original evidential limits became harder to see?

Peripheral neuropathy did not need to become impossible for the model to require revision. It did not need to be disproved for its jurisdiction to shrink. It did not need to disappear from the record for the feet to be brought more fully back inside the neurological story. What it needed was an evidential scale proportionate to what had actually been demonstrated.

The illusion ends, if it ends at all, when those scales are restored.
